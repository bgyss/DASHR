Introduction

Background

DASHR is an idea I’ve had kicking around in my head for a long time to combine the power of volumetric representations and the flexibility of raytrace-style traversal, but with the locality and cache-friendliness of the standard triangle-based rendering pipelines.

At a macro scale, the existing standard gamedev pipeline of a mesh of triangles with 4-bone-per-vertex skinning, an albedo and normal map, and maybe also some more advanced BRDF-style shading is extremely powerful. Artists have become extremely proficient in authoring this sort of model. It has obvious artificial pain points such as “doing your UVs” - mapping the texture onto the mesh in an elegant way without distorting the mapping or wasting too much space. On the other hand, artists have become very very good at this, and there are various tools to help, and once done they do provide an intuitive and useful model for performance and quality.

When rendered, triangle-based methods have been susceptible to huge advances in hardware accelleration in the standard pipelines for Direct3D, OpenGL, Vulkan and Metal. Huge effort has been put into making these triangle meshes fast and cache-friendly. Triangle mesh rendering is also well-supported in a very different rendering architecture in the “tile-based rendering” style of hardware acceleration - just as fast, just as clever, and now ubiquitous in mobile rendering devices.

Overall, it would be folly to depart too far from the absolutely tried-and-tested and blisteringly quick implementations of the traditional triangle mesh pipeline.

And yet meanwhile we have a series of elegant volumetric representations of data that are loosely lumped together in the “raycasting” category. This includes triangle representations, but also Signed Distance Field reprentations beloved of democoders, myriad traversal algorithms in infinite Shadertoy demos, voxel representations, fractal representations, the creations from Media Molecule’s Dreams, the world of The Tomorrow Children, and whatever amazing thing pours out of Inigo Quilez’s head this week.

It would be glorious to be able to use these far more versatile techniques and representations, but they have some fundamental barries to adoption in the existing gamedev pipelines:

  • How do people author this content?
  • How can the data be rendered on wide range of existing hardware?
  • How can we keep the data localised and moderately cache-coherent for performance?
  • How do you animate and manipulate this data?
  • How does this data interact with existing assets and toolchains?
  • How do levels-of-detail work for less capable hardware, or longer draw distances?
  • How can this content be streamed?
  • How can we use all the work done before, and not reinvent the wheel?

All these problems crop up as soon as anything with the word “raytracer” starts being considered. Some games, such as Dreams and The Tomorrow Children, faced this problem head-on and indeed reinvented many wheels, and sometimes with amazing effect! But it is a long and rough road, I am not smart or arrogant enough, and I cannot in good conscience act as Pied Piper leading my fellow gamedevs into a new promised land of custom toolchains and epically-missed schedules. We need… a compromise.

Previous work

Raytracers have been around a long time. They work very well in functional terms, but their performance is typically orders of magnitude slower than equivalent rasteriser pipelines because they have poor memory coherence as they traverse their working set. In addition, it is difficult to stream that working set, putting hard limits on the size of worlds that can be raytraced without requiring huge memory stores. Additionally, most of the techniques to improve memory coherence cost even more memory, causing more cache pressure, and they end up not helping as much as hoped.

Hardware acceleration for raytracing has been gradually added to GPUs, and it has some success, but the inherently poor cache coherence limits its performance even under the best circumstances. More typically it is integrated into a conventional rasteriser pipeline, and used for shadows and reflections, and even then at a disproportionate performance cost.

Parallax Occlusion Mapping has been written about many times, but it is typically phrased in terms of breaking the surface into smaller shapes. One option is deformed triangular prisms with non-parallel sides, another is to break them into yet-smaller tetrahedrons. Once the space has been chopped into more regular and somewhat linear spaces, regular analytical raytracing methods are used within each Euclidian space. Unfortunately, authoring these meshes is cumbersome, the data structures are large, and animating them is even more difficult (in fact, I have not seen a paper that deals with animation). However, the concepts in P.O.M. are very similar and informative to the concepts in DASHR, and one can be thought of as somewhat an extension of the other, but with some of the rigidity thrown out and replaced with a lot of handwaving and iteration.

What DASHR is

Sorry, sorry - but all the academic papers insist you talk about previous work. So here’s what DASHR is, briefly, skipping over lots of details to get the big picture.

First, let me define some terms. I will try to Use Caps For These Terms to highlight these precise meanings in the text:

  • Object Space: regular old Euclidian space, centered in whatever the “object” is you are rendering - a character, a vehicle, a rock. You can do maths here, units are in meters, right angles stay right angles, parallel lines never meet, etc. Good stuff.
  • Surface Space: the thin space defined by a triangular mesh’s surface, with a certain fairly small thickness. This is also often called “tangent space” in many lighting algorithms, and it is defined by the Normal, Tangent and Bitangent basis vectors at each vertex. Note that in the context of DASHR there are subtle differences between the conventional ideas of tangent space, which I will go into later, but the mental model is still very similar. Like the lighting equivalent, Surface Space can be deformed by runtime animation.
  • Unique Mapping: a set of UV coordinates that map any point on the object to a unique spot on a texture. To be explicit, no two spots on the mesh are mapped to the same place in the texture. In addition, “gutters” are needed between disjoint parts of the mesh. This may already be a familiar concept to some - some toolchains require these for e.g. lightmap generation passes, or normal-map baking from higher-resolution assets. There are existing tools to generate these if artists are unwilling.
  • Distortion Texture: a “texture” that uses the Unique Mapping to wrap around the mesh. Each texel on this texture defines a mapping from Object Space to Surface Space for that specific point on the mesh. Although conceptually a 4x3 matrix, it can be stored in a variety of ways in practice. This texture is dynamically generated each frame.

Some of you may have guessed the Cunning Trick already! But let me go step-by-step anyway.

  • Take a conventional triangle-based mesh. It should be mapped with standard albedo, normal, etc maps. It can be animated with standard 4-bone-per-vertex animation. So far, this is what 99% of games already do.
  • Give the mesh a Unique Mapping UV coordinate, and also at each vertex define a normal vector. From the Unique Mapping, generate tangent and bitangent vectors. The normal, tangent and bitangent vectors now define a Surface Space at each point on the mesh, forming a “skin” with a certain thickness around the mesh.
  • Give the mesh a heightfield and a physical scale for this heightfield. This heightfield lives in Surface Space and defines the actual rendered surface, with hills valleys, etc. As with existing conventional rendering pipelines, there will usually be a normal map associated with the heightfield, used for shading.
  • A first render pass animates the mesh, computes the object-to-surface mapping at each vertex, and writes this mapping to the Distortion Texture. This is done by rendering the standard mesh, but the “screen position” is the Unique Mapping UV coordinates, and the destination is the Distortion Texture (typically mapped as multiple render targets). This is why the mapping must be unique!
  • A second “edgefill” render pass does boring stuff that I’ll talk about later. Don’t worry about it for now.
  • The third render pass animates the mesh (again), “inflates” it by the thickness of the skin along the vertex’s normal vector, and this is the position in Object Space. It also reads the Unique Mapping UV coordinates from the vertex, and the starting extrusion height of the vertex, and together they are the position in Surface Space. It renders the triangle mesh, passing both Object Space and Surface Space positions to the pixel shader.
    • Each pixel shader invocation knows where on the outer skin it starts in both Object Space and Surface Space, and (from where it is on the screen) what direction in Object Space the ray from the viewer is going.
    • The pixel shader steps the ray along its direction in Object Space. This is easy, because Object Space has right angles and doesn’t bend. The pixel shader absolutely knows where it is in Object Space at all times, because it knows where it isn’t, and where it was.
    • The pixel shader also tracks where it THINKS the ray is in Surface Space, however because Surface Space is bendy and also animated, it may be slightly wrong.
    • Surface Space is defined by the Unique Mapping (and extrusion distance), and it is where both the heightfield and the Distortion Texture live. The Distortion Texture defines the mapping from Object Space to Surface Space. But of course you need to know where you are in Surface Space to know where to read the Distortion Texture!
    • Using its best guess of its current position in Surface Space, the pixel shader reads the Distortion Texture. It then uses that to transform its position in Object Space (which it knows for sure) into a new position in Surface Space. This is hopefully a much better guess than before, and closer to the truth.
    • The raytracer progresses by making small steps in Object Space, and each step it predicts and then refines its guess of its position in Surface Space.
    • At each point, it uses its estimate of its Surface Space position to reads the heightfield. If its height in Surface Space is below the heightfield - it hit! It finds the intersection point, samples the albedo and normal maps, and computes lighting in all the usual ways.
    • Optionally, it can send another ray out towards the light and see if it hits another part of the mesh/heightfield. This raytrace is performed in exactly the same way, except that all it cares about is hit or miss - whether the point is in shadow or not.
    • If the ray position in Surface Space goes beyond the thickness of the skin, and outside Surface Space, it has missed the mesh entirely, and the pixel is not drawn. The standard Z-buffer will deal with “macro-scale” occlusion effects.

And that’s about it! Much of this may be familiar to folks who write raycasters or pathtracers in Shadertoy, or who have worked with Parallax Occlusion Mapping in the past. The core addition of DASHR is, I think, two key tricks:

  • The first pass uses the GPU to create, every frame, a mapping from Object Space to Surface Space at every point in Surface Space. This was previous done as an offline process (e.g. in Parallax Occlusion Mapping techniques) or as a larger whole-world acceleration structure, which is very hard to do every frame. A nice bonus is that it uses exactly the same mesh data as in the main render pass, just flipping the data around.

  • This gives us a mapping from Object Space to Surface Space, which is what we want, but to get it we need to know roughly where we are in Surface Space to be able to look the data up! Flipping the key so you can look it up from an Object Space corodinate would be great, but unfortunately Object Space is 3D and relatively large (it is a volume, not a “skin”), and filling that up every frame would be expensive. However, if we know where we are starting from in Surface Space, we can iteratively traverse through Surface Space (which is curved!) to get closer and closer to the desired Object Space using iterative gradient-descent-style approaches.

  • The raytracing step is not an analytical raytracer, it is more of an iterative path tracer, as used in Signed Distance Field representations. These inherently sample their environment and iterate towards a solution, rather than computing it directly. Why look at that - to traverse Surface Space, we also need an iterative approach. The cunning (and fortunate) trick is that both types of iterations can be done at the same time. We can iterate through the volumetric data representation (heightfields, SDF, voxels, fractals, etc) AND iterate through the curved Object Space to Surface Space mapping at the same time.

This last step was the biggest unknown - could I do both iterations at the same time, maybe using a few more steps than necessary, or would the criteria for one clash with the criteria for the other and cause the estimates to diverge wildly and uselessly. As it turns out - it seems to work remarkably well in most common cases!

Details

This section will go through the details of each phase in a chronological order. Because some of the preprocessing in the early phases is only used later on, it may be a little tricky to read in linear order, and some hopping around may be required.

It also introduces two concepts not covered in the brief overview above:

  • Edgefill Texture: this is a texture generated at start of day that is mapped onto the mesh using the same Unique Mapping as used by the Distortion Texture. The function of this texture is to flood fill outwards from the valid mapped part of the Unique Mapping, and replicate data into the unmapped parts - to “fill in the gutters” so that all parts of the map have plausible (if not necessary correct) data. Each texel has two channels that describe the UV coordinate of where in the Unique Mapping this texel should pull its data from. Most texels inside the valid mesh will simply store their own UV coordinates, resulting in no data movement. Texels beyond the edge of the mesh will hold the UV coordinate of the nearest valid texel, spreading valid data outwards and filling in the gutters. This is a fairly standard concept in many texturing techniques, e.g. when producing mipmaps.

  • Teleport Texture: this is a texture generated at start of day that is mapped onto the mesh using the same Unique Mapping as used by the Distortion Texture. Each texel has two channels and a “valid” flag. If the “valid” flag is set, then other two channels supply UV coordinates of another place in the Unique Mapping. The job of this texture is to tell the raytracer “if you got here, you should be over there” and it joins places in the Unique Mapping together. This makes seams work - when the raytracer steps off the edge of the mesh (at a seam) in Surface Space, the Teleport Texture will tell it where to go to find the corresponding bit of Surface Space on the other side of the mesh seam.

These two textures are somewhat complementary - they both tell the raytracer what to do when it steps off the edge of the mesh in Unique Mapped Surface Space. The Edgefill Texture ensures that there is valid data “out there” so that linear interpolation can do the right thing, and so that stepping more than a texel off the edge doesn’t give junk data. At the same time, the Teleport Texture redirects the raytracer and tells it where to find the adjoining part of the map in Surface Space.

Author the mesh

This is the simplest phase, as it is much like any other conventional pipeline. The mesh has a few restrictions that need to be followed:

  • A unique UV map must be provided - that is, each part of the UV map must correspond to only one part of th emesh (or no part).
    • The simplest answer is for an artist to supply this through the standard UV mapping interfaces of DCC tools.
    • There are also some tools to generate this unique mapping, although they might not support some of the other requirements.
      • This unqiue mapping is a common concept, and often required for techniques such as generating lightmaps.
  • Sufficient “gutter” space in the UV mapping between edges.
  • No T-junctions. This is a common requirement for 3D meshes in general.
  • All triangles must be the same winding direction (CW/CCW) in UV space (this could be relaxed)
  • All edges must have have exactly two triangles sharing them, possibly a across a seam.
  • Smooth normals are required across seams. This could be improved with a smarter mesh preprocessor and/or future work.
    • Sharp and lone edges could be supported with extra “cliff edge” geometry, and is needed for objects such as clothing. I plan to work on this soon.

In the current demo, all texture channels (albedo, normal map, heightfield) also use this same unique UV mapping, however this is not required, and they could use one or more secondary UV channels.

It is also assumed that the mesh is specified in standard D3D/OGL/Metal format of having a triangle speficied by three indices, each of which indexes into a vertex buffer which has all the information required. Where two different UV mappings meet, what I call a “seam”, the vertices on that seam will be duplicated. They will be two different vertices in the buffer - they will have the same position and normal data, but they will have different UV, tangent and bitangent data. Most DCC tool output is significantly more complex, with each triangle specified but multiple indices (one for each of position, normal, UV coord, etc) - this could be supported with effort.

The incoming mesh does not need valid tangent and bitangent data - that will be generated. As the demo currently works, it does require normals to be provided, and they need to be smooth across seams. However, with future effort the normals could be computed from the mesh and some sort of smoothing group data.

Render the mesh to find which texels in the Distortion Texture are not covered, generate the Occupancy Map

Most of the following preprocessing phase is done on the CPU, but this phase is done on the GPU. To find out which of the texels are updated by the per-frame Distortion Pass, we do an initital Distortion Pass render. The target is cleared with distinctive data (here I use INFinity, since at the moment the Distortion Texture is all float32 data, the the Distortion Pass is done. The texture is copied back to the CPU, and where data is still INFinity, we know that this texel was not updated by the Distortion Pass. This is marked in a map (in the demo, this is the bool surfaceWarpPresent member in the TeleportEdgefillData array). This Occupancy Map is used later on in preprocessing.

Find prox vertices

The vertices of the mesh are scanned to find what I call “prox” vertices - vertices that have the same position in space, but may differ in UV coordinate. As the demo currently works, they are also required to have the same normals, but future work should relax this. Prox vertices are placed in a circular linked list, each pointing to the next.

Find matching edges

The triangles are scanned to make sure every edge in the mesh has exactly one matching edge, either using the same vertices, or proximal vertices with the same positions, but maybe different UV coordinates. Note that it is important to support “V-shaped” seams - those are seams where one vertex is actually shared between the edges, but the other vertices are proximal to each other. These are a common feature of real-world mesh UV construction to avoid excessive UV distortion.

If an edge is a true edge, in that the triangles on both sides use the same vertices, no futher action is necessary. If an edge is a “seam” where the vertices used by the triangles on each side share position but not UV coordinates, more work is required.

Draw teleports

The Teleport Texture tells the raytracer how to traverse in Surface Space from one side of a seam to the other side. Each seam found in the previous phase is traversed in the Unique Mapping space, and for each texel on or near the seam edge, we calculate what the closest point on the edge is, and thus where the UV coordinate of the other side of seam is. This UV coordinate is written into the Teleport Texture. This process is done twice, one for each direction of crossing the seam.

For simplicity, the current demo does not attempt to walk the seam edges precisely with Bresenham or equivalent - it simply draws a bounding box around the edge, computes the distance to the edge, and keeps the values closest to an edge. This makes sure no texels are missed, and combined with the next flood fill stage, works surprisingly well.

Where the Occupancy Map tells us that the Distortion Texture will have valid data, rendered each frame, we do not need to teleport - we are inside the mesh, and the data is valid! So the Teleport Texture is kept set to “invaid” at those texels. It is only the texels beyond the edges that are valid, and cause the raytracer to teleport.

Draw edgefill

Where the Occupancy Map tells us that the Distortion Texture will have valid data, the Edgefill Texture’s UV source is just set to its current UV value, i.e. the source and destination are the same, no data movement required. Where the Occupancy Map is clear, the Edgefill Texture remains invalid, ready for the next phase.

Flood-fill teleport and edgefill

For each currently invalid texel in the Edgefill and Teleport Textures that is NOT set in the Occupancy Map, the nearest valid data is copied. This effectively does a flood fill of the Edgefill and Teleport Textures to fill in all the areas not covered by the Distortion Pass. This phase is currently implemented inefficiently in the demo - it could be sped up, but hey it’s a preprocess step.

Generate tangent and bitangent data for the mesh

The incoming mesh should have had valid possition, normal and UV data, but did not need valid tangent and bitangent data. Although there are industry-standard tools (such as the excellent MikkTSpace) to generate these spaces, they typically generate tangent spaces where the tangent and bitangent are unit-length, and at right angles to each other. This is typically fine when using tangent space for normal-map lighting, and compresses well to octahedral maps and suchlike.

However the DASHR algorithm requires tangent and bitangent vectors to be physical lengths and to accurately point along lines of constant U and V, since they form the mapping from Surface Space to Object Space. The best way to visualise them is to take a single triangle, consider the texture mapped on to it, and extend that plane out in space until the whole texture is shown from 0.0 to 1.0 in both U and V space. This will be a parallelogram - and quite a large one in most cases! The tangent vector is the vector along the “U” side of the parallelogram, and the bitangent vector is along the “V” side of the parallelogram. They are not necessarily at right-angles to each other (though they should be at right-angles to the normal), nor are they necessarily the same lengths.

In practice, the tangent and bitangent are only well-defined for each triangle of the mesh. For each vertex, we find a weighted average of the tangent and bitangent of the adjacent triangles. Here I weight by the angle the triangle forms at the vertex, which seems to work well.

As the demo currently works, the normals for each vertex are taken from the source mesh, while the tangent and bitangent are computed as above. Pedantically, the normal should be at right angles to both the tangent and bitangent (though they are not necessarily at right angles to each other). However, the algorithm is robust to all three not being at right angles, and there doesn’t seem to be any real need to ensure this.

Also note that if the normals were re-calculated to be at right-angles to tangent and bitangent, it adds extra complity since proximal vertices do need to have matching normals, but they will (by definition) not share tangent and bitangent data. This means that you would need to calculate a normal that was the average of all the proximal vertices, then go back into each and recalculate the tangent and bitangent so they are at right angles to this new average normal. It’s a lot of work, and it does not appear to be necessary to make the algorithm work.

Finish preprocssing

Discard the Occupancy Map, turn the Teleport and EdgeFill Textures into actual GPU textures (they will not be updated frame-to-frame) & discard the source data.

Now on to the work done at each frame.

Render Distortion Texture #1

The first pass renders to the Distortion Texture. Because it is a Unique Mapping, no Z buffering or blending is needed. The mesh rendered is exactly the same mesh data as the main render pass, and the vertex shader is very similar.

First the vertex shader reads all the usual data from the vertices, and performs standard 4-weighted-bone animation as used is almost every existing game engine, to produce animated & deformed values for position, normal, tangent and bitangent. These vectors are all now in Object Space.

Limitations and subtleties

There are many! I will try to point out which are fundamental limits, which are tricky parts that could be improved with some effort, and which are bits I just haven’t done because of lack of time, or because there were more exciting things to tackle first.

  • The algorithm as-is requires a few things, some of which could be fixed with effort (see discussions below):

    • A unique UV map for all faces.
    • Sufficient “gutter” space in the UV mapping between edges.
    • No T-junctions. This is a common requirement for 3D meshes in general.
    • All triangles must be the same winding direction (CW/CCW) in UV space (this could be relaxed)
    • All edges must have have exactly two triangles sharing them, possibly a across a seam.
    • Smooth normals are required across seams. This could be improved with a smarter mesh preprocessor.
      • Sharp and lone edges could be supported with extra “cliff edge” geometry, and is needed for objects such as clothing. I plan to work on this soon.

  • The lengths of the tangent and bitangent matter because they help the ray travel through surfeace space. You can cope to an extent with wrong lengths, but it will need to do more iterations.

    • Sadly this means existing tangent-generation tools such as MikkTSpace are usually not suitable for tangent-spec generation, since they produce normalized tangent and bitangent vectors. However they could probably be modified to support this option.

  • Discontinuities in tangent or bitangent (including in length) will disrupt things oddly and cause more iterations.

    • Severe ones will cause “tornado poles” to appear where the ray gets horribly confused and spirals around.
    • It is usually better to force a seam and add a teleport in these cases.

  • Because the Teleport Texture must be sampled with point sampling, seams that are diagonal or not along texel boundaries can be visible if the albedo/normal/height maps are not precisely matched. It may be reasonable to express the seam more precisely as a slope+offset edge and bake these into a side-channel in the Teleport Texture to improve this.

  • Self-shadowing stops if the ray goes outside the skin volume, which means for fully correct self-shadowing (e.g. an arm casting shadow on a leg) it should be combined with traditional shadowbuffer techniques. This is more an observation than a restriction. A nice thing about doing the local self-shadowing with raytracing is that the epsilons on the shadowbuffering designed to prevent acne and peter-panning can be relaxed considerably, as it only needs to start to work at significant distance from the surface.

  • Currently the unique UV map determines everything - albedo, normal map, heightfield, and tangent space used for shading. This doesn’t have to be the case, and those “regular” textures could all use a different set of UVs which is not unique. However, if you do this, care needs to be taken about where in the code you mean which set of UV/tangent spaces, and don’t get them mixed up.

  • If the mesh distortion is pushed too hard, e.g. at the “poles” of the wiggly tube, or if the size of the animation is cranked up, or if the thickness of the skin is made too large, the raytracer sees “skyghosts” and “tornados” floating outside the mesh that I am puzzled by. I need to track these down, understand them, and fix them.

  • There are many cases where mesh seams are somewhat ugly and the artists would be expected to fix them by making sure the albedo and heightfields match on either side of the seam. This is also a problem for the current conventional art pipeline of course, but it’s made more obvious by adding a heightfield. Good robust solutions to this should be invetigated.

Future work

I decided to publish this super early. There is still much to do before this is bomb-proof, but I wanted to get the core concepts out there for folks to try out and play with, even if only in their heads. So I focused on the parts that were big unknowns, and worrying me, to prove they would work with relatively general, flexible meshes from conventional contemporary art pipelines.

Most of the rest of the work is “just typing” in that while it’s important (e.g. all the compression work will be needed to get good practical performance, which after all is one of the main points of this work), it is relatively known quantities with likely few surprises. Then again, this is graphics - there’s always surprises.

I plan to continue working on this for a bit, but I hope others will join me in this exploration. That said, I am not looking for Pull Requests - this is my hobby, not a job. I am more than happy to swap war stories over social media, or discuss gnarly corner cases, or cunning solutions to those corner cases.

In scope

  • Instead of finding the inverse of surfaceFromObject to do the shading step, you could get the initial distortion pass to store it in a separate texture.

  • Instead of doing the extra edgefill copy step, you could add mesh “fins” to write the extra parts in a single pass.

    • You could also possibly do a CS, which can bind a target as read&write.

  • In theory, you could do teleports just by having the new surfaceFromObject data at the edges, instead of using a separate teleport texture. However, because it’s a discontinuity, and we sample surfaceFromObject with interpolation, that won’t work at all well. So you probably do need the teleport indirection.

  • The raytracer does linear interpolation from the previous step when it hits the surface, which is fine, but it could instead do a binary chop for even better precision. It could do the same when it teleports.

  • Larger steps and/or more reliable route-finding could be done by comparing the surfaceFromObject matrix sampled with the previous one. If the difference is beyond some given threshold, take a smaller step and try again.

  • A lot of data - texture, vertex, etc is stored as big fat float32 data. This is just for ease of development on my part - I wanted to focus on algorithmic puzzles, not constantly go chasing down precision problems. Fairly straightforward to improve, and will give big performance benefits.

  • There’s a lot of random tweakables that had to be tuned by eye - epsilons and such, especially for the shadows. I would like to automate more of them, but we all know rendering is full of epsilons - especially shadow rendering.

  • Currently the surfaceFromObject mapping is stored as a 4x3 matrix. This is mathematically sound, but linear interpolation of the components does not produce fantastic results, because they interrelate to each other in complex ways. I want to change it to be stored in a different way so that the individual components are more separate, and interpolating each spearately produces more sensible results. This should also make using smaller data formats easier, as each component will have a better-defined range and precision.

Out of scope

  • T-junctions, interpenetrating triangles, self-intersecting meshes and other strange topologies. In many cases it’s not even clear how to define “surface space” for these topologies.

  • More “volumetric” representations that have solid insides rather than just skins. This would allow things like cutting people in half with a sword and showing their skeleton inside. It’s cool, but the representation is radically different, it burns a lot of memory, and it’s not clear how to integrate them with existing art pipelines. I shall leave this to others.

  • More general support for SDF, fractal or volumetric rendering. The heightfield tracer I have written is very simple, and heightfields are a relatively understandable representation, but we still have trouble authoring content for them. Authoring SDF or volumes in the current art pipelines feels like a very difficult challenge. Games such as Dreams have tackled this problem head-on and made amazing things, but it’s radically different to our existing pipelines, and it is unclear how to merge them.

    • That said, a major problem continues with how to animate characters in these other pipelines, and it would be fascinating to see if similar tricks of using the GPU to render warps from one space to the other could be used in those contexts to add animation to their pipelines.
