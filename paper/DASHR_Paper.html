Introduction

Background

DASHR is an idea I’ve had kicking around in my head for a long time. I wanted to combine the power of volumetric representations and the flexibility of raymarch-style traversal, but with the locality and cache-friendliness of the standard triangle-based rendering pipelines.

At a macro scale, the existing standard gamedev pipeline of a mesh of triangles with 4-bone-per-vertex skinning, an albedo and normal map, and maybe also some more advanced BRDF-style shading is extremely powerful. Artists have become extremely proficient in authoring this sort of model. It has some obvious pain points such as “doing your UVs” - mapping the texture onto the mesh in an elegant way without distorting the mapping or wasting too much space. On the other hand, artists have become very very good at this, and there are various tools to help, and once done they do provide an intuitive and useful model for performance and quality.

When rasterised with a depth buffer, triangle-based methods have been susceptible to huge advances in hardware acceleration in the standard pipelines for Direct3D, OpenGL, Vulkan and Metal. Huge effort has been put into making rendering these triangle meshes fast and cache-friendly. Triangle mesh rendering is also well-supported in a very different rendering architecture in the “tile-based rendering” style of hardware acceleration - just as fast, just as clever, and now ubiquitous in mobile devices.

Overall, I am wary of departing too far from the absolutely tried-and-tested and blisteringly quick implementations of the traditional triangle mesh pipeline.

And yet meanwhile we have a series of elegant volumetric representations of data that are loosely lumped together in the “raycasting” category. This includes triangle representations, but also Signed Distance Field representations beloved of democoders, myriad traversal algorithms in infinite Shadertoy demos, voxel representations, fractal representations, the creations from Media Molecule’s Dreams, the world of The Tomorrow Children, and whatever amazing thing pours out of Inigo Quilez’s head this week.

It would be glorious to be able to use these far more versatile techniques and representations, but they have some fundamental barriers to adoption in the existing gamedev pipelines:

  • How do people author this content?
  • How can the data be rendered on the wide range of existing hardware?
  • How can we keep the data localised and moderately cache-coherent for performance?
  • How do you animate and deform this data?
  • How does this data interact with existing assets and toolchains?
  • How do levels-of-detail work for less capable hardware, or longer draw distances?
  • How can this content be streamed from disk or network?
  • How can we reuse the existing tools and not reinvent the wheel?

All these problems crop up as soon as anything with the word “raytracer” or “raymarcher” starts being considered. Some games, such as Dreams and The Tomorrow Children, faced this problem head-on and indeed reinvented many parts of the authoring and rendering pipeline, and sometimes with amazing effect! But it is a long and rough road, I am not smart or arrogant enough, and I cannot in good conscience act as Pied Piper leading my fellow gamedevs into a new promised land of custom toolchains and epically-missed schedules. We need… a compromise.

Previous work

Raytracers have been around a long time. They work very well in functional terms, but their performance is typically orders of magnitude slower than equivalent rasteriser pipelines because they have poor memory coherence as they traverse their working set. In addition, it is difficult to stream that working set, putting hard limits on the size of worlds that can be raytraced without requiring huge memory stores. Additionally, most of the techniques to improve memory coherence cost even more memory, causing more cache pressure, and they end up not helping as much as hoped.

Hardware acceleration for raytracing has been gradually added to GPUs, and it has some success, but the inherently poor cache coherence limits its performance even under the best circumstances. More typically it is integrated into a conventional rasteriser pipeline, and used for shadows and reflections, and even then at a disproportionate performance cost. All the current implementations require a significant up-front cost to build a spatial acceleration structure (usually a Bounding Volume Hierarchy of some kind) and this is a big barrier to raytracing animated shapes.

Raymarching is closely related to raytracing, and the terminology is inexact, but the way I use the two terms, a raytracer attempts to find a one-shot analytic answer to where on the object an infinitely long ray hit, and find the answer in one go. In contrast a raymarcher steps along the ray piece by piece, evaluating a function to see if something was hit along the way. This allows them to iteratively solve intersections that cannot be solved analytically, such as with most complex Signed Distance Functions, and raymarchers are used in many fantastic demos and Shadertoy programs. There are of course many hybrids of the two approaches, and as said the terminology is fluid. DASHR is very much a raymarcher and not a raytracer, and in this case the iterative nature is core to the algorithm.

Parallax Mapping has been written about and implemented many times and many ways. The idea is to map a heightfield to a standard realtime mesh and then raytrace or raymarch through the heightfield as part of the pixel shader. This has the advantage of rendering the same mesh as used for regular rendering pipelines, and so using many of the existing creation tools. The limits of this technique are that silhouette edges are not handled correctly, for example as shown in this video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6f37CFbJ9k0 and means that the effect breaks down when the size of the heightfield becomes significant compared to the size of the model, so it is only useful for relatively small displacements. Dealing with texture seams is tricky for similar reasons.

Parallax Occlusion Maps / Shell Maps are an extension of parallax mapping for both runtime and offline raytracing. The idea is to model the object as a standard mesh, but then extrude the mesh slightly into a thicker “shell” or “skin”, then chop that shell into smaller chunks - typically prisms or tetrahedra. These smaller primitives then have a low enough curvature that they can be rendering with conventional raytracing/raymarching methods. An example of this, generating first prisms and then tetrahedra, is the paper Shell Maps, Porumbescu, Budge, Feng, Joy: https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~misha/ReadingSeminar/Papers/Porumbescu05.pdf The biggest limitation is the large number of tetrahedra generated, and the difficulty of generating them under dynamic deformation or animation - it is not clear how to do this generation quickly, or with GPU acceleration.

DASHR’s algorithm is best considered an evolution of Parallax Mapping, and adds many of the concepts of Shell Mapping, such as the idea of an extruded shell of triangles. However it does not produce extra prism/tetrahedra geometry, and does not attempt to locally linearise the problem. It also, aside from some preprocessing, runs entirely on the GPU.

Nonlinear Ray Tracing for Displacement and Shell Mapping, Shinji Ogaki, SigGraph Asia 2023: https://github.com/shinjiogaki/nonlinear-ray-tracing. Like Shell Mapping, it starts by chopping the surface into distorted prisms, but it requires fewer prisms as it raytraces through them with curved lines, solving rational cubic equations rather than straight lines. Note that because this paper starts from the perspective of raytracing, it spends a fair bit of time talking about the spatial acceleration structure, and it is unclear how to move this algorithm to an efficient realtime technique.

What DASHR is

Super TL;DR

  • Take a standard skinned mesh with a unique UV mapping. The unique UV mapping defines Surface (shell/skin) Space on the mesh.

  • Have a uniquely-mapped Distortion Texture that stores the transform from Object Space to Surface Space for each place on the mesh’s skin.

  • Each frame, animate the mesh with usual 4-bone skinning, calculate the Object Space to Surface Space transform at each vertex, and render it as a mesh to the Distortion Texture. The “pixel coordinate” is the unique UV mapping, i.e. the position in Surface Space.

  • Then render the mesh to the regular framebuffer, extruded by the skin thickness, and each pixel shader invocation is a raymarcher.

  • The raymarcher traces in Object Space. It reads the Distortion Texture to let it transform from Object Space to (distorted, animated) Surface Space. Now it knows where it is in Surface Space, it can read the heightfield to see if/where it hit.

  • Then the raymarcher uses this new position in Surface Space to read the next step of the Distortion Texture. As it steps along in linear Object Space, reading the Distortion Texture lets it transform that position to a new position in curvy Surface Space.

  • Repeat until the raymarcher hits the heightfield, or exits without hitting it.

  • …and there’s lots of details about how to deal with mesh edges and seams.

A less-brief summary

First, let me define some terms. I will try to Use Caps For These Terms to highlight these precise meanings in the text:

  • Object Space: regular old Euclidean space, centred in whatever the “object” is you are rendering - a character, a vehicle, a rock. You can do maths here, units are in meters, right angles stay right angles, parallel lines never meet, etc. Good stuff.

  • Surface Space: the space defined by a triangular mesh’s surface, extruded by a certain thickness. This is also often called “tangent space” in many lighting algorithms, and it is defined by the Normal, Tangent and Bitangent basis vectors at each vertex. Note that in the context of DASHR there are subtle differences between the conventional ideas of tangent space, which I will go into later, but the mental model is still very similar. This is also called “shell space” or “skin space” in some literature. Just like the lighting equivalent, Surface Space can be deformed by runtime animation in standard ways.

  • Unique Mapping: a set of UV coordinates that map any point on the mesh surface to a unique spot on a texture. To be explicit, no two spots on the mesh may be mapped to the same place in the texture, and vice versa. In addition, “gutters” are needed between disjoint parts of the mesh. This is a familiar concept in gamedev - some toolchains require these for e.g. lightmap generation passes, or normal-map baking from higher-resolution assets. There are existing tools to generate these if artists are unwilling.

  • Distortion Texture: a “texture” (or usually a set of textures) that uses the Unique Mapping to wrap around the mesh. Each texel on this texture defines a mapping from Object Space to Surface Space for that specific point on the mesh. Although conceptually a 3x4 matrix, it can be stored in a variety of ways in practice. The DASHR demo uses three textures, each with four channels, but it can also be thought of a single 12-channel texture. This texture is dynamically generated each frame.

    Note that the UV coordinates used to look up values in the Distortion Texture are the definition of Surface Space, so it may seem odd that it stores the mapping from Object Space to Surface Space - if I needed to know where I was in Surface Space to look up this transform, I would already know where I am in Surface Space! Which is true. But the idea is the raymarcher knows approximately where it is in Surface Space, and can look up the transform, and hopefully the transform field is smooth enough that it can then use the transform to find a better approximation of where it is in Surface Space. This iterative loop is the core of DASHR.

Then, to implement DASHR, step-by-step:

  • Take a conventional triangle-based mesh. It should be mapped with standard albedo, normal, etc maps. It can be animated with standard 4-bone-per-vertex animation. So far, this is what 99% of games already do.

  • Give the mesh a Unique Mapping UV coordinate, and also at each vertex define a normal vector if not already present. From the Unique Mapping, generate tangent and bitangent vectors. The normal, tangent and bitangent vectors now define a Surface Space at each point on the mesh, forming a “skin” with a certain thickness around the mesh.

  • Give the mesh a heightfield and a physical scale for this heightfield. This heightfield lives in Surface Space and defines the actual rendered surface, with hills and valleys. As with existing conventional rendering pipelines, there will usually be an albedo and normal map associated with the heightfield, used for shading.

    So far, this is very similar to techniques such a Parallax Mapping.

  • A first render pass animates the mesh with the various bones in a conventional way, computes the Object Space to Surface Space transformation at each vertex. It then passes these to the pixel shader which interpolates and writes this transformation to the Distortion Texture. This is done by rendering the standard mesh, but the “screen position” is the Unique Mapping UV coordinates, and the destination is the Distortion Texture (typically mapped as multiple render targets). This is why the mapping must be unique - each texel in the Distortion Texture should be a unique point on the mesh surface, and written to only once.

  • A second “edgefill” render pass does stuff that I’ll talk about later. Don’t worry about it for now.

  • The third render pass animates the mesh with the bones (again), “inflates” the mesh by the thickness of the skin along the vertex’s normal vector, and this is the vertex’s position in Object Space. It also reads the Unique Mapping UV coordinates from the vertex, and the starting extrusion height of the vertex, and together they are the position in Surface Space. It renders the triangle mesh, passing both Object Space and Surface Space positions to the pixel shader.

    • Each pixel shader invocation knows where on the outer skin it starts in both Object Space and Surface Space, and (from where it is on the screen) what direction in Object Space the ray from the viewer is going.
    • The pixel shader steps the ray along its direction in Object Space. This is easy, because Object Space has right angles and doesn’t bend. The pixel shader absolutely knows where it is in Object Space at all times, because it knows where it isn’t, and where it was.
    • The pixel shader also tracks where it thinks the ray is in Surface Space, however because Surface Space is bendy and also animated, it may be slightly wrong.
    • Surface Space is defined by the Unique Mapping UV and by the height in that space, and it is where both the heightfield and the Distortion Texture live. The Distortion Texture defines the mapping from Object Space to Surface Space. But of course you need to know where you are in Surface Space to know where to read the Distortion Texture!
    • Using its best guess of its current position in Surface Space, the pixel shader reads the Distortion Texture. It then uses that to transform its position in Object Space (which it knows for sure) into a new position in Surface Space. This is hopefully a much better guess than before, and closer to the truth.
    • The raymarcher progresses by making small steps in Object Space, and each step it predicts and then refines its guess of its position in Surface Space.
    • At each point, it uses its estimate of its Surface Space position to read the heightfield. If its height in Surface Space is below the heightfield - it hit! It finds the intersection point, samples the albedo and normal maps, and computes lighting in all the usual ways.
    • Optionally, it can send another ray out towards one or more lights and see if it hits another part of the mesh/heightfield. This raymarch is performed in exactly the same way, except that all it cares about is hit or miss - whether the point is in shadow or not.
    • If the ray position in Surface Space goes beyond the thickness of the skin, and outside Surface Space, it has missed the mesh entirely, and the pixel is not drawn. This creates correct silhouettes, and the standard Z-buffer will deal with “macro-scale” occlusion effects.

Much of this may be familiar to folks who write raycasters or pathtracers in Shadertoy, or who have worked with Parallax Mapping in the past. The core addition of DASHR is, I think, four key tricks:

  • The first pass uses the GPU to create, every frame, a mapping from Object Space to Surface Space at every point in Surface Space. This was previously done in other techniques as an offline process (e.g. in Parallax Occlusion Mapping techniques) or as a larger whole-world acceleration structure, which is very hard to do every frame. A nice bonus is that it uses exactly the same mesh data as in the main render pass, just flipping the data around.

  • This gives us a mapping from Object Space to Surface Space, which is what we want, but to get it we need to know roughly where we are in Surface Space to be able to look the data up! Flipping the key so you can look it up from an Object Space coordinate would be great, but unfortunately Object Space is 3D and relatively large (it is a volume, not a “skin”), and filling that up every frame would be expensive. However, if we know where we are starting from in Surface Space, we can iteratively traverse through Surface Space (which is curved!) to get closer and closer to the desired equivalent of our Object Space using iterative approaches.

  • The raymarching step is an iterative path tracer, as used in many Signed Distance Field representations. These inherently sample their environment and iterate towards a solution, rather than attempting to compute it analytically. This is a perfect synergy - to traverse Surface Space, we also need an iterative approach. The cunning (and fortunate) trick is that both types of iterations can be done at the same time. We can iterate through the volumetric data representation (heightfields, SDF, voxels, fractals, etc) and iterate through the curved Object Space to Surface Space mapping at the same time.

This last step was the biggest unknown - could I do both iterations at the same time, maybe using a few more steps than necessary, or would the criteria for one clash with the criteria for the other and cause the estimates to diverge wildly and uselessly. As it turns out - it seems to work remarkably well in most common cases. Phew!

The fourth trick is how to deal with seams in the mesh where the mesh is smooth but the UV mapping has discontinuities or large distortions. The next section goes into the details of this.

Details

This section will go through the details of each phase in execution order. Because some of the preprocessing in the early phases is only used later on, it may be a little tricky to read in linear order, and some hopping around may be required.

It also introduces two concepts not covered in the brief overview above:

  • Edgefill Texture: this is a texture generated at start of day that is mapped onto the mesh using the same Unique Mapping as used by the Distortion Texture. The function of this texture is to flood fill outwards from the valid mapped part of the Unique Mapping, and replicate data into the unmapped parts - to “fill in the gutters” so that all parts of the map have plausible (if not necessary correct) data. Each texel of the edgefill texture has two channels that describe the UV coordinate of where in the Unique Mapping this texel should pull its data from. Most texels inside the valid mesh will simply store their own UV coordinates, resulting in no data movement. Texels beyond the edge of the mesh will hold the UV coordinate of the nearest valid texel, spreading valid data outwards and filling in the gutters. This gutter-filling is a fairly standard concept in many texturing techniques, e.g. when producing mipmaps or lightmaps.
  • Teleport Texture: this is a texture generated at start of day that is mapped onto the mesh using the same Unique Mapping as used by the Distortion Texture. Each texel of the teleport texture has two channels and a “valid” flag. If the “valid” flag is set, the other two channels supply UV coordinates of another place in the Unique Mapping. The job of this texture is to tell the raymarcher “if you got here, you should be over there” and it joins places in the Unique Mapping together. This makes seams work - when the raymarcher steps off the edge of the mesh (at a seam) in Surface Space, the Teleport Texture will tell it where to go to find the corresponding bit of Surface Space on the other side of the mesh seam.

The edgefill and teleport textures are somewhat complementary - they both tell the raymarcher what to do when it steps off the edge of the mesh in Unique Mapped Surface Space. The Edgefill Texture ensures that there is valid data “out there” so that linear interpolation can do the right thing, and so that stepping more than a texel off the edge doesn’t give junk data. At the same time, the Teleport Texture redirects the raymarcher and tells it where to find the adjoining part of the map in Surface Space.

Now you have those concepts, the runtime steps to implement DASHR (or at least the sequence used in the demo) are as follows:

Preprocessing phase

Author the mesh

This is the simplest phase, as it is much like any other conventional pipeline. The mesh has a few restrictions that need to be followed:

  • A unique UV map must be provided - that is, each part of the UV map must correspond to only one part of the mesh (or no part).
    • The simplest answer is for an artist to supply this through the standard UV mapping interfaces of DCC tools.
    • There are also some tools to generate this unique mapping, although they might not support some of the other requirements.
      • This unique mapping is a common concept, and often required for techniques such as generating lightmaps.
  • Sufficient “gutter” space in the UV mapping between edges.
  • No T-junctions. This is a common requirement for 3D meshes in general.
  • All triangles must be the same winding direction (CW/CCW) in UV space (this could be relaxed)
  • All edges must have have exactly two triangles sharing them, possibly a across a seam.
  • Smooth normals are required across seams. This could be improved with a smarter mesh preprocessor and/or future work.
    • Sharp and lone edges could be supported with extra “cliff edge” geometry, and is needed for objects such as clothing. I plan to work on this soon.

In the current demo, all texture channels (albedo, normal map, heightfield) also use this same unique UV mapping, however this is not required, and they could use one or more secondary UV channels, and their mapping does not need to be unique.

It is also assumed that the mesh is specified in standard D3D/OGL/Metal format of having a triangle specified by three indices, each of which indexes into a vertex buffer which has all the information required. Where two different UV mappings meet, what I call a “seam”, the vertices on that seam will be duplicated. They will be two different vertices in the buffer - they will have the same position and normal data, but they will have different UV, tangent and bitangent data. Most DCC tool output is significantly more complex, with each triangle specified but multiple indices (one for each of position, normal, UV coord, etc) - this could be supported with effort. However, most realtime rendering systems already have pipelines that convert to the D3D/OGL/Metal style.

The incoming mesh does not need valid tangent and bitangent data - that will be generated. As the demo currently works, it does require normals to be provided, and they need to be smooth across seams. However, with future effort the normals could be computed from the mesh and some sort of smoothing group data.

Generate the Occupancy Map to find which texels in the Distortion Texture are covered

Most of the following preprocessing phase is done on the CPU, but this phase is done on the GPU. To find out which of the texels are updated by the per-frame Distortion Pass, we do an initial Distortion Pass render. The target is cleared with distinctive data (here I use INFinity, since for the demo the Distortion Texture is all float32 data). Then the standard runtime Distortion Pass (see below) is done, using any reasonable animation data. All we care about is which texels get data written to them. The texture is copied back to the CPU, and where data is still INFinity, we know that this texel was not updated by the Distortion Pass. This is marked in a map (in the demo, this is the bool surfaceWarpPresent member in the TeleportEdgefillData array). This Occupancy Map is used later in preprocessing.

Find prox vertices

The vertices of the mesh are scanned to find what I call “proximal” vertices - vertices that have the same position in space, but may differ in UV coordinate. As the demo currently works, they are also required to have the same normals, but future work should relax this. Prox vertices are placed in a circular linked list, each pointing to the next.

Find matching edges

The triangles are scanned to make sure every edge in the mesh has exactly one matching edge, either using the same vertices, or prox vertices with the same positions, but different UV coordinates. Note that it is important to support “V-shaped” seams - those are seams where one vertex is actually shared between the edges, but the other vertices are proximal to each other. These are a common feature of real-world mesh UV construction to avoid excessive UV distortion. In the demo they occur near the ends of the “wiggly tube” mesh.

If an edge is a true edge, in that the triangles on both sides use the same vertices, no further action is necessary. If an edge is a “seam” where the vertices used by the triangles on each side share position but not UV coordinates, more work is required.

Draw teleports

The Teleport Texture tells the raymarcher how to traverse in Surface Space from one side of a seam to the other side. Each seam found in the previous phase is traversed in the Unique Mapping space, and for each texel on or near the seam edge, we calculate what the closest point on the edge is, and thus where the UV coordinate of the other side of seam is. This UV coordinate is written into the Teleport Texture. This process is done twice, one for each direction of crossing the seam.

For simplicity, the current demo does not attempt to walk the seam edges precisely with Bresenham or equivalent - it simply draws a bounding box around the edge, computes the distance to the edge, and keeps the values closest to an edge. This makes sure no texels are missed, and combined with the next flood fill stage, works surprisingly well.

Where the Occupancy Map tells us that the Distortion Texture will have valid data, updated every frame, we do not need to teleport - we are inside the mesh, and the Distortion Texture data is valid. So the Teleport Texture simply points back at itself and is marked as “invalid” at those texels. It is only the texels beyond the edges that are valid, and cause the raymarcher to teleport.

In this diagram, the insides are just drawn with their own UV coordinates (red = U, green = V), and do not cause teleportation. It is only the edges with the different colours that actually cause any teleportation.

Draw edgefill

Where the Occupancy Map tells us that the Distortion Texture will have valid data, the Edgefill Texture’s UV source is just set to its current UV value, i.e. the source and destination are the same, no data movement required. Where the Occupancy Map is clear, the Edgefill Texture remains invalid, ready for the next phase. Note that the inside of the edgefill and inside of the teleport textures are the same data (stored UV = actual UV), so these phases could be combined.

At the moment, the edgefill texture does nothing interesting - it needs the flood-fill phase to work.

Flood-fill teleport and edgefill

For each currently invalid texel in the Edgefill and Teleport Textures that is NOT set in the Occupancy Map, the nearest valid data is copied. This effectively does a flood fill of the Edgefill and Teleport Textures to fill in all the areas not covered by the Distortion Pass. This phase is currently implemented inefficiently in the demo - it could be sped up, but it is just a preprocess step, not done every frame.

The teleport texture can be hard to interpret. Each seam edge has an inside where the Distortion Texture is valid and no teleportation is needed. The UV coordinates here are just the UV coordinates of the texel, i.e. if the teleport was done, it would have no effect. On the other side of the seam, outside the valid Distortion Texture, the teleport texture’s UV values are the UV values of the inside of the matching seam elsewhere in the Distortion Texture.

In the following diagram, pairs of edges are highlighted with different coloured ellipses. Focusing just inside the white and yellow ellipses, it should be clear that their contents are the same colours and shapes. So for the white set, in both cases orange is on the left, green on the right. The same for the yellow ellipses. One side of the teleport texture says “stay here” while the other side says “go to the matching one”, thus the colours match in both areas.

In the case of the cyan ellipses, the same is true even though the orientation of the ellipses is different. There is still a burgundy colour on one side of the seam, and a green-to-orange gradient on the other.

Generate tangent and bitangent data for the mesh

The incoming mesh should have had valid position, normal and UV data, but did not need valid tangent and bitangent data. Although there are industry-standard tools (such as the excellent MikkTSpace) to generate these spaces, they typically generate tangent spaces where the tangent and bitangent are unit-length, and at right angles to each other. This is typically fine when using tangent space for normal-map lighting, and compresses well to octahedral maps and suchlike.

However the DASHR algorithm requires tangent and bitangent vectors to have lengths that mean physical values, and to accurately point along lines of constant U and V, since they form the mapping from Surface Space to Object Space. The best way to visualise them is to take a single triangle, consider the texture mapped on to it, and extend that plane out in space until the whole texture is shown from 0.0 to 1.0 in both U and V space. This will be a parallelogram - and quite a large one in most cases! The tangent vector is the vector along the “U” side of the parallelogram (from V=0.0 to V=1.0), and the bitangent vector is along the “V” side of the parallelogram (from U=0.0 to U=1.0). They are not necessarily at right-angles to each other (though they should be at right-angles to the normal), nor are they necessarily the same lengths, and usually neither is unit-length.

In practice, the tangent and bitangent are only well-defined for each triangle of the mesh. For each vertex, we find a weighted average of the tangent and bitangent of the adjacent triangles. Here I weight by the angle the triangle forms at the vertex, which seems to work well.

As the demo currently works, the normals for each vertex are taken from the source mesh, while the tangent and bitangent are computed as above. Pedantically, the normal should be at right angles to both the tangent and bitangent (though they are not necessarily at right angles to each other). However, the algorithm is robust to all three not being at right angles, and there doesn’t seem to be any real need to ensure this.

Also note that if the normals were re-calculated to be at right-angles to tangent and bitangent, it adds extra complexity since proximal vertices do need to have matching normals, but they will (by definition) not share tangent and bitangent data. This means that you would need to calculate a normal that was the average of all the proximal vertices, then go back into each and recalculate the tangent and bitangent so they are at right angles to this new average normal. It’s a lot of work, and it does not appear to be necessary to make the algorithm work.

Most existing pipelines will have tools to generate tangent spaces, and it should be relatively simple to modify them to produce the correct “physical” tangent and bitangent vectors that DASHR requires, either instead of or in addition to the standard ones used for normal-map lighting. The DASHR demo demonstrates using the physical vectors for conventional normal-map lighting.

Finish preprocessing

Discard the Occupancy Map, turn the Teleport and EdgeFill Textures into actual GPU textures & discard the source data. Both textures are computed only in this preprocessing step and do not need to be updated during normal rendering. They are currently stored using float32 channels for simplicity, but since both store UV coordinates for the Distortion Texture, a smaller format of 2x unorm16 would work instead.

Now on to the work done at each frame.

Realtime phase

Render Distortion Texture #1

The first pass renders to the Distortion Texture. The HLSL for this pass is in PipelineDeform.hlsl in the demo code. Because it is a Unique Mapping, no Z buffering or blending is needed. The mesh rendered is exactly the same vertex & index buffer data as the main render pass, however it is used slightly differently.

The important thing about this pass is that the destination position of the vertex (and then the destination of the pixel shader) is not the position in Object Space (or a perspective projection of it), as it is in the main render pass. Instead, the destination “pixel” coordinate is the Unique Space UV mapping, read directly from the vertex buffer, and the place it is rendering to is the Distortion Texture (or the multiple textures that it consists of).

First the vertex shader reads all the usual data from the vertices, and performs standard 4-weighted-bone animation as used in almost every existing game engine, to produce animated & deformed values for position, normal, tangent and bitangent. These vectors are all now in Object Space, i.e. they have been animated to the correct object-local space. This space is Euclidean, not distorted, and you can do standard geometry in it.

However at each vertex the Unique Mapping UV, normal, tangent and bitangent form the basis vectors for Surface Space. This is where the heightfield is defined in, and the vertex shader can define a transformation (conventionally thought of as a 3x4 matrix) from Surface Space to Object Space, formed from these basis vectors. Note the direction here - the normal, tangent and bitangent are vectors in Object Space, and so if given any point in Surface Space (e.g. a heightmap texel), they can transform the point to Object Space. The naming system in the demo code tries to be very specific here - this transform is called objectFromSurface. The “from” nomenclature is used so that when writing multiplication the names match up and provide some error checking - you can read more about it in my blog post here: https://tomforsyth1000.github.io/blog.wiki.html#%5B%5BMatrix%20maths%20and%20names%5D%5D

However, what we want to write into the Distortion Texture is the inverse. The raymarcher will know where it is in Object Space - that is where the ray is marching through in a straight line. What it wants to know is where that is in Surface Space, so this phase inverts that 3x4 matrix to produce the SurfaceFromObject transform. This is then written to the Distortion Texture. The slightly goofy part is that as previously mentioned, the position this transform is written to in the Distortion Texture (i.e. the destination pixel coordinate in the render-target) is the coordinate in Surface Space, formed by the Unique Mapping UV.

Yo dawg - I heard you wanted to know where you were in Surface Space, so I gave you a map in Surface Space that gives you a transform to Surface Space so you could find out where you are in Surface Space.

In practice what happens is the raymarcher always knows roughly where it is in Surface Space, and the distortion is smooth enough that the transform it looks up is close enough that when applied to the known Object Space, it gives a more accurate Surface Space position. This breaks down in some cases, which I will explore later.

The pixel shader in this pass is trivial - it interpolates the transform and writes it to the Distortion Texture, which is mapped as three rendertargets, each made of four float32 components. The only thing to note is that while the transforms calculated by the vertex shader are accurate, they will be component-wise-interpolated when being passed to the pixel shader, which is not necessarily an accurate thing to do. This is discussed in the “future work” section.

Render Distortion Texture #2 to perform edge-filling

The previous pass only wrote to the Distortion Texture texels actually covered by the mesh. However, as the raymarcher marches around the surface, it can stray off the mesh slightly, and it is also using bilinear interpolation, so we need to “flood fill” out a bit.

To do this a pass renders from Distortion Texture #1 to Distortion Texture #2 using the edgefill texture as an indirection. For texels that are updated by the previous distortion render pass, the edgefill texture is simple straight-through mapping. For texels outside the mesh, the edgefill texture indirects to the nearest texel that is updated.

The shader code is in PipelineEdgefill.hlsl and is very short. It is fed a single quad that covers the whole destination.

Note that this pass renders from Distortion Texture #1 to a different Distortion Texture #2 because for most graphics pipelines you cannot bind a surface as both a source and a destination at the same time, so unfortunately some of this memory is a simple copy, which is a waste of bandwidth. One solution would be to use a compute shader pass instead, and only update the texels that need to be copied.

Final main render pass

This phase renders the mesh to the visible framebuffer with the regular depth buffer, projection matrices and all the usual parts of a render pipeline.

The shader code is in PipelineMain.hlsl. The vertex shader is very conventional. It reads the vertices and animates the position and normal with 4-bone-weighted-skinning like a conventional pipeline. For ease of reading it also animates the tangent and bitangent, although these are not used in this phase.

The mesh is then extruded by moving the vertex position outwards along the vertex normal by half the skin thickness, and the vertices go through the standard camera and projection matrix to get mapped to the screen.

The vertex shader then calculates the direction in Object Space of a ray being sent from the camera to the vertex. This will be passed to the raymarcher in the pixel shader.

That’s all for the vertex shader - very simple. The pixel shader is where the big work happens. However, most of the actual code in the ps_main() function is concerned with lighting and debugging. These are not particularly unique to DASHR and aside from the two calls to TraceRay() should be fairly obvious. There is a little subtlety to preventing self-shadowing acne controlled by shadowAcneScaler but the comments should explain it well.

The most important interpolated values passed from the vertex shader to the pixel shader are the Object Space ray start and ray direction, and also the ray start position in Surface Space. Because the pixel shader was invoked on the inflated surface of the mesh, it knows the starting Surface Space position because that is simply the interpolated Unique Mapping UV, combined with a height of 1.0.

After that, the bulk of the work in the pixel shader is done by the call to the function TraceRay(). This gets passed in a ray start position and direction in Object Space, the start position in Surface Space, and a variety of controls and scales. It has a big loop that does the following steps. The ones in bold are the heart of the DASHR algorithm:

  • Remember the current position and distance above the heightfield.
  • Find the ray’s Object Space position using the start and direction and the current distance travelled.
  • Use the Surface Space position to check the teleport texture to see if a mesh seam needs to be teleported across.
    • If so, the current position in Surface Space is updated from the teleport texture.
  • Use the Surface Space position to read the surfaceFromObject transform from the Distortion Texture.
    • Note this is still the previous iteration’s Surface Space position.
  • Calculate the new Surface Space position from the surfaceFromObject transform and the new Object Space position.
    • This also calculates the Surface Space direction. This is not currently used, but it may be useful in future.
  • If the ray has escaped the Surface Space “skin” volume without hitting the surface, return.
  • Use the Surface Space position to read the heightfield.
    • Compare to the Surface Space position’s height.
    • If the ray has hit, interpolate between the previous and current positions to get slightly better precision, and return.
  • Increase the ray distance travelled by an amount related to how far above the heightfield you still are.

Many of these steps are a standard part of any raymarcher, and there is nothing particularly clever about this implementation of it, and it could certainly be improved in many ways - but it is good enough to prove the technique works.

The key innovations of DASHR are the three steps highlighted in bold. Using a rough guess of the Surface Space position, using that to get an approximate transform from Object Space to Surface Space, and using that to refine the new Surface Space position.

The other key performance realisation is that the refinement of Surface Space position can be done at the same time as the inherent raymarching iteration loop. While you are far from the surface, precision is not as important, so larger steps can be made. As you approach the surface, smaller steps need to be made, and the Surface Space precision estimate naturally improves in quality.

It may turn out that in some cases, these two refinements need to be done separately, or that the refinement loop needs to become smarter (see the Future Work section for more discussion), but for the majority of pixels rendered, this very simple loop works surprisingly well.

The TraceRay() function is invoked once for the primary ray intersection. If it hits the surface, some basic lighting is done, and if the surface is facing the light, a second call to TraceRay() checks to see if the surface is shadowed by other parts of the surface. This is a very simple lighting routine, but it serves to demonstrate the flexibility of the core TraceRay() functionality.

Limitations and subtleties

There are many! I will try to point out which are fundamental limits, which are tricky parts that could be improved with some effort, and which are bits I just haven’t done because of lack of time, or because there were more exciting things to tackle first.

  • The algorithm as-is requires a few things, some of which could be fixed with effort (see discussions below):

    • A unique UV map for all faces.
    • Sufficient “gutter” space in the UV mapping between edges.
    • No T-junctions. This is a common requirement for 3D meshes in general.
    • All triangles must be the same winding direction (CW/CCW) in UV space (this could be relaxed)
    • All edges must have have exactly two triangles sharing them, possibly a across a UV seam.
    • Smooth normals are required across seams. This could be improved with a smarter mesh preprocessor.
      • Sharp and lone edges could be supported with extra “cliff edge” geometry, and this ability is needed for objects such as clothing. I plan to work on this soon.

  • The lengths of the tangent and bitangent matter because they help the ray travel through surface space. You can cope to an extent with wrong lengths, but it will need to do more iterations.

    • Sadly this means existing tools such as MikkTSpace are usually not suitable for tangent-space generation, since they produce normalised tangent and bitangent vectors. However they could probably be modified to support this option.

  • Discontinuities in tangent or bitangent (including in length) will disrupt things oddly and cause more iterations.

    • Severe ones will cause “tornado poles” to appear where the ray gets horribly confused and spirals around.
    • It is usually better to force a seam and add a teleport in these cases.

    TODO: picture of a tornado pole.

  • Surfaces can bend in on themselves, either due to mesh construction and extrusion, or because of animation with large amounts of bending. When this happens, the Surface Space self-intersects. In general DASHR’s algorithm uses the current Surface Space position, looks up the Object To Surface transform with it, and then transforms the new Object Space position by this transform to get a new Surface Space position. The assumption is that this will be closer to the real Surface Space position than the last one. However, when Surface Space inverts because of self-intersection, this no longer holds true - the new guess can be worse than the previous one, and as the raymarch continues, the Surface Space guess might never converge to the correct one.

    In practice what this leads to is that self-intersecting areas (e.g. the inside of a sharply-bent elbow) have two “ends” that you can look into them from, and you can see the surface just continuing on through the actual surface, as if little or no bending had actually occurred, making a sort of “tunnel”. In general this is not that objectionable - no geometry sticks out - but it does look odd on close inspection.

    TODO: picture of this effect.

    Hopefully, some smarter iterative measures will resolve this. For example, the raymarcher could notice that the new guess is worse than the previous one, and start looking all around it in multiple directions and finding the best approximation, rather than just moving in a straight line. This is one of the more urgent areas I plan to explore.

  • Meanwhile, on the outer edge of large animation distortion, beyond a certain curvature the surface starts to bulge out in strange ways, and “knuckles” and “sky ghosts” can start appearing. The exact cause of this is unknown at the moment, and again this is an urgent area of research. My hunch is that the current formulation of the Object To Surface transform is not ideal. Although mathematically correct, doing component-wise linear interpolation is not a sound operation. This happens in the first pass when rendering the Distortion Texture (when the transforms are passed from the vertex shader to the pixel shader), and then in the main pass when the Distortion Texture is read with linear interpolation by the texture unit. There is a different way to express the transform that I think should interpolate more robustly, and this may improve matters significantly.

    TODO: image of knuckles and sky-ghosts.

  • Because the Teleport Texture must be sampled with point sampling, seams that are diagonal or not along texel boundaries can be visible as jaggies if the albedo/normal/height maps are not precisely matched. It may be reasonable to express the seam more precisely as a slope+offset edge and bake these into a side-channel in the Teleport Texture to improve this.

  • Self-shadowing stops if the ray goes outside the local skin volume, which means for fully correct longer-range self-shadowing (e.g. an arm casting shadow on a leg) it should be combined with traditional shadowbuffer techniques. This is more an observation than a restriction - I do not plan to try to solve this, as long-range tracing of shadow rays has many performance implications. A nice thing about doing the local self-shadowing with raymarching, but transitioning to shadowbuffers for the medium and long range shadows is that the epsilons on the shadowbuffering designed to prevent acne and peter-panning can be relaxed considerably, as it only needs to start to work at significant distance from the surface.

  • Currently the unique UV map determines everything - albedo, normal map, heightfield, and tangent space used for shading. This doesn’t have to be the case, and those “regular” textures could all use a different set of UVs which is not unique. However, if you do this, care needs to be taken about where in the code you mean which set of UV/tangent spaces, and don’t get them mixed up - I have not been particularly careful in the labelling here.

  • If the mesh distortion is pushed too hard, e.g. at the “poles” of the wiggly tube, or if the size of the animation is cranked up, or if the thickness of the skin is made too large, the raymarcher sees “sky-ghosts” and “tornados” floating outside the mesh that I am puzzled by. I need to track these down, understand them, and fix them.

  • There are many cases where mesh seams are somewhat ugly and the artists would be expected to fix them by making sure the albedo and heightfields match on either side of the seam. This is also a problem for the current conventional art pipeline of course, but it’s made more obvious by adding a heightfield. Good robust solutions to this should be investigated.

Future work

I decided to publish this super early. There is still much to do before this is bomb-proof, but I wanted to get the core concepts out there for folks to try out and play with, even if only in their heads. So I focused on the parts that were big unknowns, and worrying me, to prove they would work with relatively general, flexible meshes from conventional contemporary art pipelines.

A lot of the rest of the work is “just typing” in that while it’s important and significant effort, it is relatively known techniques with few surprises. As an example, a lot of compression work will be needed to get good practical performance - which after all is one of the main points of this work - but exploring this space is familiar to many people. Then again, this is graphics - there’s always surprises.

However some of the work still involves head-scratchers, especially the behaviour around areas of high distortion, especially animation. These are the top priorities for me.

I plan to continue working on this for a bit, but I hope others will join me in this exploration. That said, I am not looking for Pull Requests - this is my hobby, not a job. Please view this as a research paper, a proof-of-concept demo, and a starting point for copy-paste into your own pipelines - it is not intended to be a practical “live” library. That said, I am more than happy to swap war stories over social media, or discuss gnarly corner cases, or cunning solutions to those corner cases. And if anybody does want to turn it into a real library - go for it!

In scope

  • Investigate mitigations for “knuckles” and “sky-ghosts” in areas of high animation distortion.

  • Investigate mitigations for “tornados” in areas of high distortion.

  • Currently the surfaceFromObject mapping is stored as a 3x4 matrix. This is mathematically sound, but linear interpolation of the components does not produce fantastic results, because they interrelate to each other in complex ways. I want to change it to be stored in a different way so that the individual components are more separate, and interpolating each separately will produce a more sensible result. This should also make using smaller data formats easier, as each component will have a better-defined range and precision.

  • Instead of finding the inverse of surfaceFromObject to do the shading step, you could get the initial distortion pass to store it in a separate texture.

  • Instead of doing the extra edgefill copy step, you could add mesh “fins” to write the extra parts in the first pass.

    • You could also possibly do a CS, which can bind a target as read&write.

  • In theory, you could do teleports just by having the new surfaceFromObject data at the edges, instead of using a separate teleport texture. However, because it’s a discontinuity, and we sample surfaceFromObject with interpolation, that won’t work at all well. So you probably do need the teleport indirection.

  • A lot of data - texture, vertex, etc is stored as big fat float32 data. This is just for ease of development on my part - I wanted to focus on algorithmic puzzles, not constantly go chasing down precision problems. Fairly straightforward to improve, and will give big performance benefits.

  • The raymarcher does linear interpolation from the previous step when it hits the surface, which is fine, but it could instead do a binary chop for even better precision. It could do the same when it teleports.

  • Larger steps and/or more reliable route-finding could be done by comparing the new surfaceFromObject transform with the previous one. If the difference is beyond some given threshold, take a smaller step and try again.

  • There’s a lot of random tweakables that had to be tuned by eye - epsilons and such, especially for the shadows. I would like to automate more of them, but we all know rendering is full of epsilons - especially shadow rendering.

Out of scope - at least for me

  • T-junctions, interpenetrating triangles, self-intersecting meshes and other strange topologies. In many cases it’s not even clear how to define “surface space” for these topologies. I do want to support lone edges and thin sheets, e.g. for clothing, but arbitrary triangle soup is not a goal.

  • More efficient raymarching and acceleration structures. Better lighting models. Ambient occlusion. Subsurface scattering. Other fancy effects. All those things are great of course, and if you adapt DASHR to your engine you should totally do those improvements, but there’s plenty of other papers and tutorials about them, especially in the SDF/Shadertoy/raymarcher scene - go read them! The raymarcher in this demo is just good enough to show the technique working, and that’s all it needs to be.

  • More “volumetric” representations that have solid insides rather than just skins. This would allow things like cutting objects in half with swords or lightsabers and showing their skeleton inside. It’s cool, but the representations are radically different, they burn a lot of memory, and it’s not clear how to integrate them with existing art pipelines. I shall leave this problem to others.

  • Making DASHR a general robust library that you can drop into your own pipeline. This is a research paper and a proof-of-concept demo. I do not have the time or bandwidth to make this a public library. I shall leave this to others.

  • More general support for SDF, fractal or volumetric rendering. The heightfield tracer I have written is very simple, and heightfields are a relatively understandable representation, but we still have trouble authoring content for them. Authoring SDF or volumes in the current art pipelines feels like a very difficult challenge. Games such as Dreams have tackled this problem head-on and made amazing things, but it’s radically different to our existing pipelines, and it is unclear how to merge them.

    • That said, a major problem continues with how to animate characters in these other pipelines, and it would be fascinating to see if similar tricks of using the GPU to render warps from one space to the other could be used in those contexts to add animation to their pipelines. I think this is an area with lots of interesting research avenues.
